# Module 7 - Evaluation
<!-- RE2026 -- > <!-- ACMSIGMOBILE -- > <!-- BeyerWinter2025 -->

Artifact evaluation is a formal process through which the research community verifies that published artifacts are long-term available, documented, functional, and reusable. Understanding how evaluation works and what reviewers expect helps authors prepare artifacts that are not only technically sound but also easy to assess.

Reviewer feedback should be treated as an opportunity to improve artifact quality, clarify assumptions, and strengthen reproducibility, not as an adversarial assessment. A critical baseline: artifacts should be usable without direct interaction with the authors.

# Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

- Understand artifact review models, anonymization requirements, and submission workflows.
- Prepare artifacts that meet reviewer expectations for usability, documentation, and resource requirements.
- Support reviewer validation by mapping artifact outputs to paper claims and providing reproducible execution paths.
- Understand artifact evaluation criteria, badge systems, and quality expectations.
- Respond effectively to reviewer feedback during the clarification process.

# Review models and anonymization

> **Tip:** Artifact anonymization requirements may differ from paper anonymization requirements. Always verify the specific policies of the target venue regarding artifact visibility, URLs, repository identities, and reviewer anonymity before preparing the submission. <!-- SPLASH2024-CfA -->

## Review models

| Review model | Description | Artifact implications |
|---|---|---|
| **Open review** | Authors and reviewers know each other's identities. | Artifacts can be shared publicly or via standard links. |
| **Single-anonymous** | Reviewers know authors' identities, but not vice versa. | Author identity in the artifact is not a concern, but reviewer anonymity must be protected. |
| **Double-anonymous** | Neither authors nor reviewers know each other's identities. | Authorship information must be removed from the artifact and all associated metadata. |

## Anonymization for double-anonymous review

The general anonymization principles and identifier types were covered in [Module 2 - Preparation](content/M2.md). For artifact submission specifically:

When double-anonymous review is required:

- Remove author names from all project files, scripts, and documentation. <!-- Survey2026- -->
- Remove metadata that may reveal authorship (e.g., file properties, commit history, author fields in configuration files). <!-- Survey2026- -->
- Remove identifying information from repository titles, descriptions, and screenshots. <!-- Survey2026 -->
- Ensure that identifying information is removed not only from files and datasets, but also from repository metadata. <!-- Graziotin2019 -->

Tools and strategies for anonymized artifact sharing: <!-- Survey2026 -->

- **Separate anonymized repository:** create a new, identity-free repository for submission.
- **Platform-supported temporary anonymization:** some repositories (e.g., FigShare) support private sharing links before public release.
- **Anonymous Docker repositories:** for containerized artifacts.
- [**Anonymous GitHub**](https://anonymous.4open.science/): a tool that creates an anonymized mirror of a GitHub repository.

> **Warning:** During double-anonymous review, avoid repository configurations or external services that may expose reviewer identities through access logs, analytics tools, or tracking mechanisms. <!-- ETAPS2025 -->

## Providing access during review

Many repositories support anonymous sharing links or access tokens that allow reviewers to inspect artifacts without publicly releasing them. <!-- AdarEtAl2017 -->

- When sharing during review, include the private link in the paper submission and clearly indicate it is intended for editors and reviewers only. <!-- Researchdata2026 -->
- When submitting the final version, remove the private review link and replace it with the public archival link. <!-- Researchdata2026 -->
- If the artifact depends on a live web instance or online service, ensure it remains available throughout the entire review period. <!-- NDSS2026 -->
- If confidentiality prevents public sharing, some venues accept a private or password-protected link. Avoid services such as Google Drive; use permanent repositories where no registration is required. <!-- ICSA2025- -->

# Artifact badge systems

Badge systems communicate the quality and availability level of evaluated artifacts. Requirements vary by venue, but categories similar to those presented below are common across many programs: <!-- MODELS2025 --> <!-- ICSE2026 -->

| Badge | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Available** | The artifact is publicly available in a durable, accessible archival repository with a PID. |
| **Functional** | The artifact has been inspected and found to be documented, consistent, complete, and exercisable. |
| **Reusable** | The artifact is carefully documented and structured to facilitate reuse and repurposing beyond the original paper. |
| **Reproduced** | The main results of the paper have been independently reproduced by a third party using the artifact. |

> **Note:** Usually an artifact can receive a Functional badge without an Available badge. For example, when industry data underlies strict non-disclosure agreements and the artifact is only accessible upon request. <!-- MODELS2025 -->

Authors are encouraged to target the **Reusable** badge as a quality goal, as it represents the highest level of artifact preparation. <!-- ICSE2026 -->

## Minimum requirements for good artifacts

**Availability:** <!-- MODELS2025  -->
- The artifact is publicly downloadable from a repository without registration.
- The artifact is hosted in a persistent repository with a stable URL.
- The artifact is associated with a PID.

**Functionality:** <!-- ACM2020 --> <!-- ICSA2025 -->

- **Documented:** an inventory of artifacts is included with sufficient description to enable them to be exercised.
- **Consistent:** the artifacts are relevant to the paper and contribute to the generation of its main results.
- **Complete:** to the extent possible, all components relevant to the paper are included. Proprietary artifacts need not be included but must be documented with instructions for obtaining them.
- **Exercisable:** scripts and software used to generate results can be successfully executed, and data can be accessed and manipulated as described.

**Reusability:** <!-- ICSE2026 --> <!-- ICSA2025 -->

- Artifacts are carefully documented and well-structured to facilitate reuse and repurposing.
- Community norms and standards for artifacts of this type are strictly adhered to.

# What reviewers typically check

## Initial checks

Before substantive evaluation begins, reviewers verify basic operability. Common problems that block evaluation: <!-- ICSA2025 -->

- Corrupted or missing files.
- Broken or inaccessible links.
- Invalid credentials or expired access tokens.
- Environments that do not start (e.g., VM fails to boot, container crashes).
- Scripts that immediately fail on the simplest example input.

## Deeper evaluation criteria

After confirming basic operability, reviewers assess whether: <!-- ICSA2025 --> <!-- BeyerWinter2025 -->

- The artifact can be executed and produces results consistent with the paper claims.
- The documentation and structure allow easy understanding and reuse.
- All artifacts relevant to the paper are available and accessible.
- The artifact is self-explanatory without requiring direct interaction with the authors.

# Preparing the artifact for reviewer evaluation

## Minimize setup friction

> **Key principle:** Reviewers have limited time and may evaluate many artifacts. Every unnecessary setup step reduces the chance of a successful evaluation. <!-- Padhye2019 --> <!-- ACMSIGMOBILE --> <!-- ICSE2026 --> <!-- Survey2026 -->

- Minimize the number of dependencies that reviewers must install.
  - Prefer widely available technologies (e.g., Git, Python, Docker) and require at most one or two of them.
- The best approach is to package the entire artifact inside a VM or container so that fine-grained dependencies are self-managed.
- All commands required to run the artifact should be explicitly listed and executable via copy-paste.
- Ideally, execution should begin after a single import of a container or VM image.

## Be realistic about resource requirements

<!-- Padhye2019 -- > <!-- SEFM2024 -->
- Aim for artifacts that can be reproduced on modest hardware, including standard laptops.
  - Do not expect reviewers to have access to large amounts of hardware resources. Artifacts requiring vast computational resources (e.g., 32 cores, 256 GB RAM or over 100 GB of data) may be impractical to evaluate. <!-- Padhye2019  --> <!-- SEFM2024  -->
- If the artifact requires publicly available benchmark data, consider providing download scripts rather than packaging all data in the submission.
- If specialized hardware is required (e.g., specific GPUs, clusters), it may be acceptable to provide remote access to a server with all required scripts and data.
  - Contact the artifact evaluation chairs early to confirm whether this is permitted.

## Declare human and compute time upfront

> **Tip:** Clarify the time required for every step in the README. Provide an overview with time estimates before going into step-by-step details. <!-- Padhye2019 -->

Example structure: <!-- Padhye2019 -->

```
# Overview
* Getting started    (10 human-minutes + 5 compute-minutes)
* Build              (2 human-minutes + 1 compute-hour)
* Run experiments    (5 human-minutes + 3 compute-hours)
* Validate results   (30 human-minutes + 5 compute-minutes)
* Reuse guide        (20 human-minutes)
```

Also document approximate resource usage per step: CPU usage, memory, disk space, and expected runtime. <!-- USENIXSec2026 -->

## Explain side effects before they occur

Whenever you ask reviewers to execute a command or script, explain what it does before they run it, including whether it: <!-- Padhye2019 -->

- Creates, modifies, or deletes files or directories.
- Accesses the internet or other external resources.
- Requires significant execution time, memory, or disk space.
- Produces expected outputs or visible changes that can be used to verify successful execution.

> **Tip:** Make each step idempotent whenever possible. Re-running the same command should produce the same result regardless of how many times it is executed. This prevents reviewers from ending up in broken states when they repeat steps. <!-- Padhye2019 -->

## Support quick validation

Provide a **Getting Started** section that allows reviewers to verify basic functionality quickly, ideally under 30 minutes. This section should: <!-- Survey2026 -->

- List any software requirements or passwords needed to access the artifact.
- Include a minimal working example or smoke test.
- Describe what output to expect if everything is working correctly.

## Support failure recovery

Design execution workflows so that failed commands can be safely re-executed after fixes are applied: <!-- Padhye2019 -->

- Avoid global, irreversible changes caused by setup steps.
- Clearly indicate which files and directories each command creates, so reviewers know what to remove and regenerate after a failure.
- Prefer workflows that allow partial re-execution rather than requiring a full restart.

## Produce results in readable formats

<!-- Padhye2019 -- >  <!-- Survey2026 -->
- Prefer CSV or Markdown over LaTeX source for **tables**.
- Auto-generate **figures and plots** from experimental results. Do not ask reviewers to manually interpret log files.
- Provide **interpretation guidance** to explain how to distinguish between correct and incorrect results.
  - Avoid presenting raw outputs without context.
- Include **archived expected outputs in a separate folder** for comparison, ensuring they are not overwritten during reviewer testing.

# Mapping claims to artifact outputs

> **Key principle:** Do not simply ask reviewers to run scripts without explaining what to expect. Cross-reference paper items with artifact outputs explicitly. <!-- Padhye2019 -->

Cross-referencing paper items with artifact outputs is identical to the mechanism detailed in [Scientific Documentation in Module 3](content/M3.md#scientific-documentation). For each claim in the paper, state whether the artifact supports it and how. Reference its location in the paper, identify the portion of the artifact that evaluates it, provide specific evaluation instructions, and explain why it is omitted if not supported.

Example validation block: <!-- Padhye2019 -->

```
# Validate results (30 human-minutes + 5 compute-minutes)

Supported claims:
- Table 1: results/tab1.csv reproduces Table 1 on Page 5.
- Figure 2: results/plot2.pdf reproduces the plot in Figure 2 on Page 8.
- Page 7, paragraph 3: "We outperform the baseline by 2x."
  See results/comparison.csv — the second column (our system) should be approximately twice the third column (baseline).

Claims not supported by this artifact:
- The claim on Page 8 requires specialized hardware not available in this package and is out of scope.
```

## Enabling approximate reproduction

Many experiments take very long to run fully. Provide alternative configurations that run with fewer resources and still produce results that approximate those in the paper: <!-- Padhye2019 -->

```
# Run experiments (~3 compute-hours)
Run ./exp.sh 6 30m 1 for 6 benchmarks, 30 minutes each, 1 repetition.
This takes ~3 hours and produces approximate results.

Run ./exp.sh 20 24h 10 for the full experiment (200 days total).
```

> **Tip:** Include pre-computed outputs from the full experiments alongside approximate results, in the same format. This allows reviewers to compare the two and build confidence that the full run would be consistent with the paper. <!-- Padhye2019 -->

Step-by-step instructions should focus on the artifact type: <!-- ETAPS2025 -->

- **Data artifacts:** cover data provenance, context, ethical and legal statements, and storage requirements.
- **Software artifacts:** provide instructions for reproducing paper claims (e.g., tables and figures) and documentation on how to use the tool.
- **Proof artifacts:** explain how the individual parts of the proof relate to the formalisms presented in the paper.

# Artifact versioning during review

Since the artifact may evolve during evaluation to address reviewer feedback, consider using a concept PID when submitting. It always points to the latest version, eliminating the need to update the link as revisions are made. Once evaluation is complete, switch to a **version-specific PID** in the camera-ready paper to ensure readers access the exact version associated with the publication. <!-- USENIXSec2026 --> <!-- BeyerWinter2025 --> <!-- WOOT2025  -->

> **Tip:** Reserve a DOI on Zenodo in advance so it can be included in the paper before the camera-ready deadline. <!-- RE2026 -->

# Clarification period and reviewer interaction

Artifact evaluation typically includes a clarification or rebuttal period during which authors can respond to reviewer concerns. <!-- ICSE2026 -->

During this period: <!-- Survey2026 --> <!-- Padhye2019 -->

- Treat reviewer feedback as an opportunity to improve the artifact, not as criticism.
- Be transparent and honest. If something is not working, say so clearly.
- Clarify which reported issues affect the paper's results and which are minor or expected limitations.
- Provide fixes or updated instructions when feasible. Create a new artifact version rather than silently modifying an existing one.
- Indicate the level of support you are willing to provide after the review period.

# Key takeaways

- Verify venue-specific policies on artifact anonymization, access, and submission requirements before preparing the submission.
- Under double-anonymous review, remove authorship information from files, metadata, commit history, and documentation.
- **Minimize setup friction:** target under 30 minutes for installation and under one hour for basic reproduction.
- **Declare human and compute time** for every step in the README, with an overview at the top.
- **Explain side effects** of every command before asking reviewers to execute it.
- **Map every paper claim explicitly** to artifact outputs. For unsupported claims, explain why.
- Provide **short-run configurations** that approximate full results for reviewers with limited time or resources.
- Use the **concept PID** during review; switch to a **version-specific PID** for the camera-ready paper.
- Respond to reviewers within the defined clarification window. Be transparent about limitations and bugs.
